LESSON 01What is Domain-Driven Design (DDD)?▾

ABeginner explanation

DDD means learning the business to gain a deep understanding of its language, and its rules before deciding how to organize complex software. It helps when many rules and teams must change the system over time.

Domain
The business problem area we are trying to understand.
DDD
A design approach that places business knowledge at the center of software modeling.

Why it matters: If rules are mixed together, one change can accidentally affect unrelated work. Business-centered models make responsibilities easier to discuss and test.

When to use it: Use DDD for complex, changing business rules. For simple CRUD (create, read, update, delete) systems, a simpler design may be enough.

BPractical example

An online shop accepts orders, processes payments, and ships goods. First ask which business decisions each activity makes; only then choose classes, APIs, and databases.

ONLINE SHOPPING
  |-- Ordering: accept / cancel order
  |-- Payments: charge / refund
  `-- Shipping: dispatch / track parcel

The model starts with what the company does, not the cloud product it uses.

CInterview question

You inherit a large retail system. What does applying DDD change about your first two weeks of discovery?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would interview sales, operations, finance, and delivery experts; walk through a purchase and a return; collect business terms and rules; and map decisions and ownership. I would identify areas whose rules change together and areas that need different models. Only after that would I propose modules or services.

Trade-off / limitation: DDD discovery costs time and access to experts. I would use it selectively in rules-heavy areas; straightforward reference-data CRUD may not justify rich modeling.

Mistake to avoid: I would not turn every business noun into a service or claim DDD means microservices.

EKey takeaways

  • Start from business behavior, not infrastructure.
  • Use domain experts to uncover rules.
  • DDD helps with complex domains, not every CRUD screen.
  • DDD is not a synonym for microservices.

LESSON 02What is a Bounded Context?▾

ABeginner explanation

A bounded context is a clear boundary within which one business model and its words have specific meanings. Ordering and Shipping may talk about the same purchase, but each needs different rules and information. A boundary tells us which model applies where.

Bounded Context
The scope where a particular domain model is valid.
Model
The concepts, data, and behavior used to represent business work.

Why it matters: It prevents unrelated teams from being forced to share one oversized model.

When to use it: Define contexts when parts of the business use different rules, vocabulary, lifecycles, or ownership.

BPractical example

Ordering manages purchase approval. Shipping manages carriers and delivery status. Both can refer to Order #1001, but they are not required to share all order fields.

[ Ordering model ]      [ Shipping model ]
  Order #1001              Parcel #P77
  buyer, items             carrier, tracking
  confirmed                in transit

One real purchase can appear as different models inside different boundaries.

CInterview question

Must each bounded context become one microservice and one database? Explain.

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: No. A bounded context defines a model boundary, not a deployment unit. I would first identify independent rules, data ownership, and team accountability. Then I might implement the contexts as modules in one application, or as services if independent release and scaling justify the cost.

Trade-off / limitation: Separate services add network latency, distributed failures, security surfaces, and eventual-consistency work. A modular monolith is often the safer first step.

Mistake to avoid: A department, API, database table, and bounded context are not automatically the same thing.

EKey takeaways

  • One context means one coherent model.
  • Contexts can exchange information.
  • Bounded context is not equal to microservice.
  • Choose boundaries from business rules, not tables.

LESSON 03What Does Boundary Mean in Bounded Context?▾

ABeginner explanation

A boundary says where a team's model and business responsibilities start and end. Ordering decides if a purchase can be placed or canceled. Payments handles the money movement. Shipping handles delivery. Each has rules it protects.

Boundary
A clear limit around the rules and model a context is responsible for.
Ownership
The team or capability accountable for maintaining the model and its contracts.

Why it matters: A clear limit reduces accidental changes and makes it easier to know whom to ask when a rule changes.

When to use it: Draw boundaries when the same application has responsibilities that evolve for different business reasons.

BPractical example

A laptop is returned. Ordering may decide whether the return meets policy. Payments executes the refund. Shipping arranges the parcel return.

Ordering ---> Payments ---> Refund result
    |
    `----------> Shipping ---> Return label

A workflow may cross several boundaries without transferring ownership of every rule.

CInterview question

Who decides refund eligibility and who transfers the money? Why not put both rules in one shared service?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would assign eligibility to the business capability that owns returns policy and execution to Payments. The workflow coordinates them through contracts. I would verify this with domain experts, because actual businesses may assign returns policy differently.

Trade-off / limitation: More boundaries introduce coordination and failure paths. Splitting two rules is useful only when their meanings, ownership, or changes are truly different.

Mistake to avoid: Do not equate bounded contexts with organizational departments. Teams and systems may cross boundaries.

EKey takeaways

  • Boundaries identify rule ownership.
  • A business workflow can cross several contexts.
  • Ownership must be validated with domain experts.
  • Avoid splitting solely by team chart.

LESSON 04Why the Same Word Can Mean Different Things▾

ABeginner explanation

Two business areas can use the same word but mean different things. An 'Order' for Ordering describes what was bought; for Shipping it may be a reference to what must be delivered. DDD lets each context choose the details relevant to its work.

Context-specific meaning
The interpretation of a business word within a particular model.
Semantic mismatch
When two areas use similar words but mean different things.

Why it matters: A universal object with every possible field often becomes hard to change and easy to misuse.

When to use it: Look for different lifecycles, fields, or rules behind familiar words such as Customer, Account, Order, or Status.

BPractical example

Ordering stores products, price, and cancellation state. Shipping stores carrier, parcel tracking, and delivery state. Both may reference Order #1001.

ORDERING: Order {items, price, confirmed}
SHIPPING:  Delivery {orderId, carrier, ETA}

The shared order ID links the work; it does not require identical domain models.

CInterview question

CRM and Billing both store 'Customer'. Should you enforce one enterprise Customer class?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would first compare meanings and lifecycle. CRM may own a prospect and contact relationship; Billing may own the liable payer and invoice address. I would keep distinct domain models if the rules differ, connect them with stable references and explicit translations, and define authoritative ownership for each fact.

Trade-off / limitation: Separate models create mapping and synchronization cost. A small stable shared identifier can help, but sharing the whole object creates change coupling.

Mistake to avoid: An enterprise-wide canonical Customer object is not automatically the cleanest solution.

EKey takeaways

  • Words get meaning from their context.
  • A shared identifier is not a shared model.
  • Avoid a giant universal domain entity.
  • Translate meanings explicitly at context boundaries.

LESSON 05How Do Bounded Contexts Communicate?▾

ABeginner explanation

Bounded contexts can communicate without using each other's private models. They exchange only the information they need through an agreed interface, such as an API call or a published event.

API
An agreed way for one system to request data or an action.
Contract
The agreed message structure, meanings, and expected behavior.

Why it matters: A stable contract lets one area change its internal implementation without forcing changes everywhere else.

When to use it: Use synchronous requests for immediate answers; use events when other areas can react after a business change.

BPractical example

Shipping finishes delivery and tells Ordering that Order #1001 was delivered. Ordering does not need to know the carrier's route-planning algorithm.

[Shipping] -- DeliveryCompleted(orderId) --> [Ordering]
     private carrier details stay in Shipping

Information crosses the boundary; private shipping rules do not.

CInterview question

When Shipping changes from FedEx to UPS, should Ordering need a release?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: Not if the published contract remains compatible and Ordering depends only on meaningful delivery facts. I would document events or APIs, version them carefully, and use contract tests so teams can release safely.

Trade-off / limitation: Synchronous calls are simple but create runtime dependency; events reduce direct timing coupling but add delay, duplicates, and monitoring needs.

Mistake to avoid: Do not expose internal database tables as the integration contract.

EKey takeaways

  • Contexts communicate via explicit contracts.
  • Keep internal details private.
  • Choose API or event based on timing needs.
  • Test compatibility before changing contracts.

LESSON 06What is Ubiquitous Language?▾

ABeginner explanation

Ubiquitous Language means business people and developers agree on words and use them consistently within one bounded context. The words appear in meetings, requirements, tests, and code.

Ubiquitous Language
Shared vocabulary within a bounded context.
Domain expert
A person who understands the actual business rules.

Why it matters: A precise shared vocabulary prevents defects caused by misunderstood business terms.

When to use it: Create it during discovery and refine it whenever requirements expose ambiguous terms.

BPractical example

Shipping agrees that 'Shipped' means a parcel left the warehouse, while 'Delivered' means receipt was confirmed. Developers use these meanings in status fields and tests.

Shipped   = handed to carrier
InTransit = moving through network
Delivered = confirmed at destination

Each status has one agreed meaning inside Shipping.

CInterview question

The business says 'Delivered' means received, but the code uses it after dispatch. How do you correct this?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would bring operations and developers together to define the status transitions, revise the glossary and code names, and create acceptance tests for each transition. If a public contract already uses the incorrect meaning, I would plan a compatible migration rather than silently changing it.

Trade-off / limitation: Shared language needs care as the business changes; not every word must mean the same thing in every bounded context.

Mistake to avoid: Changing a field label without changing the associated rule and contract does not fix semantic confusion.

EKey takeaways

  • Agree on terms with domain experts.
  • Use those terms in code and tests.
  • Different contexts may use different meanings.
  • Protect consumers when changing public status semantics.

LESSON 07What is a Subdomain?▾

ABeginner explanation

A domain is the larger business problem. A subdomain is one smaller business problem inside it. For an online shop, Ordering, Payments, Shipping, and Inventory Management are possible subdomains.

Domain
The complete business area being studied.
Subdomain
A distinct part of that business problem.

Why it matters: Subdomains help architects see business capabilities before choosing application or service boundaries.

When to use it: Use subdomain discovery when planning a new platform, modernizing a legacy system, or deciding investments.

BPractical example

An online shop needs to count laptops in stock. Inventory Management is one subdomain of online commerce.

ONLINE COMMERCE DOMAIN
  |-- Ordering
  |-- Payments
  |-- Inventory
  `-- Shipping

Inventory is a part of the problem domain, not a database technology.

CInterview question

How is a subdomain different from a bounded context? Can you always pair one with one?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: A subdomain describes a business problem; a bounded context marks where a model is valid in software. I would discover subdomains first and choose bounded contexts based on language, rules, and consistency. Their boundaries often align, but they do not have to match one-to-one.

Trade-off / limitation: Forcing a direct one-to-one mapping can hide complexity or create too many models. I would check model cohesion and integration cost before drawing lines.

Mistake to avoid: A subdomain is not automatically an existing application, module, or department.

EKey takeaways

  • Domain = broader business problem.
  • Subdomain = smaller business capability.
  • Subdomains help discovery.
  • Subdomain and bounded context are not identical.

LESSON 08What is a Core Subdomain?▾

ABeginner explanation

A core subdomain is a business area that gives a company a competitive advantage. A price-focused retailer may differentiate through Pricing. A rapid-delivery company may differentiate through Delivery Planning.

Core Subdomain
The business capability most important to differentiating the company.
Competitive advantage
A reason customers choose one provider over alternatives.

Why it matters: Core areas deserve focused expertise and tailored design because they help the business win.

When to use it: Classify a capability as core after checking the company's actual strategy, not just because its system is large.

BPractical example

A bank wins customers by approving loans in five minutes with a unique assessment method. Loan Risk Assessment is a candidate core subdomain.

BANK DIFFERENTIATOR: fast, trusted approvals
  `-- Core: Loan Risk Assessment

The uniqueness of its decision process matters more than the number of screens it has.

CInterview question

A bank says loan underwriting is core. How does that affect build-versus-buy?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would preserve control over the differentiating underwriting policy and decision model, invest in domain experts and testable invariants, and evaluate vendors only where they preserve required flexibility and compliance. I would measure approval speed, risk outcomes, and change lead time.

Trade-off / limitation: Building every component in-house is expensive; even core areas can rely on bought infrastructure or commodity tools.

Mistake to avoid: Core does not mean the largest team, the most expensive system, or mandatory custom code everywhere.

EKey takeaways

  • Core means strategic differentiation.
  • Validate core status against business strategy.
  • Invest design effort where rules differentiate.
  • Buy commodity support even within a core capability when sensible.

LESSON 09What is a Supporting Subdomain?▾

ABeginner explanation

A supporting subdomain is needed for a company to work, but is not itself its main competitive advantage. It may still require custom rules or close integration with the core business.

Supporting Subdomain
An enabling business area that is not the main differentiator.
Custom rules
Rules specific to how this company runs a process.

Why it matters: This category helps teams allocate limited budget and engineering attention sensibly.

When to use it: Use this classification when a capability supports important workflows but does not distinguish the company in the market.

BPractical example

A bank's unique loan risk method is core. Its loan document collection workflow supports approvals but may not be the bank's selling point.

Core: Loan Risk Assessment
  depends on
Supporting: Loan Document Collection

The document process matters, even if it is not why customers choose the bank.

CInterview question

Would you automatically outsource a supporting subdomain?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: No. I would compare the uniqueness of requirements, available products, integration complexity, security, and lifecycle cost. A supporting capability may still need tailored code because of regulation or unusual workflow. I would try to spend less bespoke effort there than on the core differentiator.

Trade-off / limitation: Supporting does not mean dispensable. Failure can still stop revenue or violate obligations.

Mistake to avoid: Avoid assuming only core work needs reliability, monitoring, or ownership.

EKey takeaways

  • Supporting capabilities enable the business.
  • They can require custom implementation.
  • Their importance is not the same as differentiation.
  • Use risk and requirements—not labels alone—for funding decisions.

LESSON 10What is a Generic Subdomain?▾

ABeginner explanation

A generic subdomain is a common problem that many organizations share and that existing products often solve well. Employee payroll and workplace email are examples for a typical bank.

Generic Subdomain
A common business problem with established, reusable solutions.
Buy-versus-build
The choice between adopting a product and writing custom software.

Why it matters: Recognizing generic needs helps avoid spending scarce engineering time reinventing standard tools.

When to use it: Look for trusted existing products when business requirements are standard and switching or integration risks are manageable.

BPractical example

A bank needs employee email. It can evaluate an established enterprise email product rather than create a mail platform.

Bank: Loan Risk -> custom advantage
Bank: Employee Email -> common solution

Email is necessary but does not usually define the bank's lending advantage.

CInterview question

When is buying a generic solution a poor choice?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would usually evaluate products first, checking capabilities, cost, availability, security, residency, lock-in, exit path, and integration. I might build a thin solution if available products cannot meet material constraints or the lifecycle cost is excessive.

Trade-off / limitation: Buying reduces custom development but adds vendors, contracts, migration, and sometimes restricted customization.

Mistake to avoid: Generic does not mean low risk or that procurement due diligence can be skipped.

EKey takeaways

  • Generic = widely shared problem.
  • Evaluate existing solutions first.
  • Assess vendor and integration risks.
  • Generic systems can still be business-critical.

LESSON 11What is a Domain Model?▾

ABeginner explanation

A domain model is a clear representation of business concepts, their information, their behavior, and rules. It is more than a collection of database columns.

Domain Model
The business concepts and rules represented in software.
Behavior
A business action, such as approving or canceling an application.

Why it matters: It makes important rules visible and reduces the chance that unrelated code bypasses them.

When to use it: Use richer models when business rules are complex or likely to change; simple CRUD may not need the same structure.

BPractical example

A bank's LoanApplication has an ID, amount, and status. It also has an approve() action that checks risk assessment first.

LoanApplication
  id: LA1001
  amount: 50000 USD
  status: Pending
  approve() -> checks risk passed

The model includes what the loan knows and what it is allowed to do.

CInterview question

What is the problem with LoanApplication being only a data record while any API can set its status?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: If every caller can write any status, the approval invariant may be bypassed. For a complex loan workflow I would put valid transitions in the domain model and call them through an application layer. I would test failed and successful transitions with business experts.

Trade-off / limitation: Rich domain models add classes and discipline. For trivial data entry, simple transactional code may be cheaper and clearer.

Mistake to avoid: Do not confuse an ORM row mapping with the full business domain model.

EKey takeaways

  • Models contain meaning and rules, not just fields.
  • Business operations should protect valid state.
  • Use complexity to justify richer modeling.
  • A database schema is not the full domain model.

LESSON 12What is an Entity?▾

ABeginner explanation

An entity is a business object whose identity matters over time. You can change some of its information and it remains the same object.

Entity
A domain object distinguished by its identity and continuity.
Identity
A way to tell which specific business object we mean.

Why it matters: Identity prevents two similar records from being mistaken for one business object.

When to use it: Use an entity when a business object must be tracked across changes, such as a loan application, order, or payment.

BPractical example

Loan LA1001 starts as Pending and later becomes Approved. It is still LA1001. Another loan LA1002 may have the same amount, but is different.

LA1001: 50000, Pending -> Approved
LA1002: 50000, Pending
Different identities; not the same loan.

Changes to status do not erase the first application's identity.

CInterview question

Are two loan applications with identical amount and status the same entity?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: Not necessarily. If they have different application identities, they are different entities. I would define identity scope and lifecycle carefully, especially across imports or systems that use different IDs; a shared key alone does not require shared business rules.

Trade-off / limitation: Maintaining global identifiers can increase mapping complexity. Define the authoritative issuer and deduplication logic.

Mistake to avoid: Comparing all fields for equality is not appropriate for identity-based entities.

EKey takeaways

  • Entity identity survives property changes.
  • Same attributes do not imply same entity.
  • Identity scope and lifecycle matter.
  • Same ID across contexts does not imply identical domain models.

LESSON 13What is a Value Object?▾

ABeginner explanation

A value object is defined by its values, not by an independent business identity. A Money value can contain both an amount and a currency. Two Money values with the same amount and currency are equal.

Value Object
An object identified by its attributes rather than an ID.
Immutable
Not edited in place after it is created; replace with a new value instead.

Why it matters: Value objects make small rules easier to validate and compare correctly.

When to use it: Use value objects for amounts, measurements, dates, or addresses when the context does not track them as separate entities.

BPractical example

Two different loans each contain Money(50000, 'USD'). The loans are different entities; their Money values are equal.

Money(50000, USD) == Money(50000, USD)
Money(50000, USD) != Money(50000, EUR)

Currency is part of the value, so an amount alone is not enough.

CInterview question

Why model Money as amount plus currency rather than a decimal?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: A Money value object prevents invalid comparisons between currencies and centralizes validation. I would make it immutable and require explicit conversion rules outside equality. In the loan model, replacing the loan amount creates a new Money value while preserving loan identity.

Trade-off / limitation: More small types add mapping and serialization work. Use them where they clarify real business constraints.

Mistake to avoid: An address could be a value object in Ordering but an entity in a property-management domain; classification depends on the context.

EKey takeaways

  • Value equality uses attributes.
  • Value objects have no domain identity.
  • Prefer immutability.
  • Classification depends on business meaning.

LESSON 14What is an Invariant?▾

ABeginner explanation

An invariant is a business rule that must remain true whenever a domain object is in a valid completed state. A loan cannot reach Approved unless its required risk assessment passed.

Invariant
A rule that a valid business state must always satisfy.
Validation
A check that prevents unacceptable data or actions.

Why it matters: Invariants stop callers, APIs, and background jobs from leaving business objects invalid.

When to use it: Define them when errors would create invalid financial, legal, inventory, or workflow states.

BPractical example

The bank rejects approve() if riskPassed is false, even if an API user requests Approved directly.

approveLoan():
  if riskAssessment != PASSED:
      reject approval
  else:
      status = APPROVED

The model—not just the screen—enforces the required approval rule.

CInterview question

Where should you enforce a loan approval invariant, and what about concurrent requests?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would enforce it in the domain operation so all application entry points use the same rule, and use a transaction or concurrency control to stop races from violating it. I would cover the rule with domain tests and audit rejected transitions.

Trade-off / limitation: Global invariants spanning multiple independently committed services are harder; I would redesign boundaries or use an explicit business workflow and reconciliation.

Mistake to avoid: UI-only checks are insufficient; another client or background process can bypass them.

EKey takeaways

  • An invariant defines valid state.
  • Enforce it at the domain boundary.
  • Concurrency may need transactional controls.
  • UI-only validation cannot protect the rule.

LESSON 15What is an Aggregate?▾

ABeginner explanation

An aggregate is a small consistency boundary around related domain objects and their business rules. It is designed around what must change together safely—not everything that is connected in a database.

Aggregate
A boundary for domain objects that must keep specific rules consistent in one transaction.
Consistency
The state follows its required business rules after a change.

Why it matters: A clear aggregate makes transaction scope and allowed changes explicit.

When to use it: Use aggregates when multiple related values must be protected together, such as order items and order totals.

BPractical example

A shopper changes laptop quantity from 2 to 3. In this simplified order design, Order and its items form an aggregate; the total updates from $2,000 to $3,000.

ORDER AGGREGATE
  Order #1001
    `-- Item: Laptop x3 @ $1000
  total = $3000

The business rule linking quantity and total stays consistent.

CInterview question

Would you put Customer, Order, Shipment, and Payment in one aggregate?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: Usually not. I would identify which rules must be atomically consistent and group only the data needed to enforce those rules. Order plus OrderItems may be appropriate; Payments and Shipments usually have separate lifecycles and consistency boundaries.

Trade-off / limitation: Large aggregates cause contention, long transactions, and reduced throughput. Small aggregates need inter-aggregate coordination, often with events and eventual consistency.

Mistake to avoid: An aggregate is not simply a graph of all objects with foreign keys.

EKey takeaways

  • Aggregate = transactional consistency boundary.
  • Group by invariants, not relationships alone.
  • Avoid very large aggregates.
  • Across aggregates, plan for coordination and delay.

LESSON 16What is an Aggregate Root?▾

ABeginner explanation

An aggregate root is the main entity that controls changes inside its aggregate. Other code asks the root to make a change so the root can enforce the rules.

Aggregate Root
The entity that is the allowed entry point for changing an aggregate.
Encapsulation
Keeping internal details behind controlled operations.

Why it matters: It prevents code from changing a child object and forgetting a related rule.

When to use it: Use it for every aggregate so changes pass through one clearly responsible object.

BPractical example

The customer wants one fewer laptop. The application calls Order.changeQuantity(itemId, 1); Order checks the state and recalculates the total.

Application
  -> Order.changeQuantity(itemId, 1)
       -> checks rules
       -> updates OrderItem
       -> recalculates total

The child item is not directly edited by the API.

CInterview question

Why should an OrderItem quantity not be changed directly from the HTTP controller?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: The Order root owns invariants such as valid quantities, allowed order states, and total calculation. I would expose intentional operations on Order, keep child collections controlled, and persist the aggregate consistently.

Trade-off / limitation: This creates more method design work than raw table updates. It pays off in complex domains with important rules.

Mistake to avoid: Do not make an aggregate root a huge coordinator for other bounded contexts; it should guard its own aggregate.

EKey takeaways

  • The root is the update entry point.
  • It protects aggregate invariants.
  • Expose business actions, not unrestricted setters.
  • Do not turn roots into system-wide orchestrators.

LESSON 17What is a Domain Event?▾

ABeginner explanation

A domain event records a meaningful fact that has already happened in a business model. 'OrderPlaced' describes a completed action; 'PlaceOrder' is a request, not a completed fact.

Domain Event
A meaningful past event in the domain.
Handler
Code that reacts to an event without being embedded in the original business action.

Why it matters: Events express important business facts and separate follow-up behavior from the action that triggered it.

When to use it: Use a domain event when other logic inside the same domain or application should react to a completed change.

BPractical example

After an order is placed, an OrderPlaced domain event can trigger internal follow-up behavior. The notification application may later send a confirmation email.

Command: PlaceOrder
  -> Order is created
  -> Domain Event: OrderPlaced
  -> Internal handler reacts

The event is a past-tense statement about an actual business change.

CInterview question

How would you distinguish a command from a domain event and avoid partial failure?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: A command asks for work; a domain event says meaningful work happened. I would raise the event from the domain operation and decide whether handlers must join the local transaction or run later. I would define the failure semantics rather than assuming all handlers succeed automatically.

Trade-off / limitation: Immediate handlers are easier to reason about transactionally but increase coupling and latency; deferred processing needs reliable delivery and recovery.

Mistake to avoid: Do not use 'OrderPlaced' before the order really exists, or treat every technical log message as a domain event.

EKey takeaways

  • Events describe the past; commands request action.
  • Events communicate meaningful facts.
  • Choose dispatch timing intentionally.
  • Domain events do not by themselves guarantee cross-service delivery.

LESSON 18What is an Integration Event?▾

ABeginner explanation

An integration event communicates a committed business change to another bounded context or system. It often travels asynchronously through a messaging system.

Integration Event
A published fact intended for another system or bounded context.
Asynchronous
The sender and receiver do not need to finish work at the same instant.

Why it matters: It lets other contexts react without reading or changing the producer's private data directly.

When to use it: Use it when several applications need to learn about a change and immediate request-response is unnecessary.

BPractical example

Payments records a successful payment and later publishes PaymentCompleted. Ordering updates its payment view; Shipping may use an approved delivery-release event depending on business policy.

Payments: commit payment
  -> PaymentCompleted integration event
  -> Ordering consumes
  -> Ordering updates its own view

The sender reports a durable fact, and each consumer decides how to react.

CInterview question

What happens if Payments commits but publishing PaymentCompleted fails?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would avoid a fragile database-write-plus-broker-send sequence. A transactional outbox can persist payment state and an event record atomically, then publish with retries. Consumers must handle duplicates and stale or out-of-order messages, with monitoring and reconciliation.

Trade-off / limitation: Events improve loose coupling but add eventual consistency, schema evolution, and operational complexity. An API may be simpler for immediate queries.

Mistake to avoid: A domain event and a public integration event need not share the same payload, name, or lifecycle.

EKey takeaways

  • Integration events cross system boundaries.
  • Publish only committed business facts.
  • Plan duplicates, lag, and recovery.
  • Do not expose private aggregate structures as public events.

LESSON 19What is a Context Map?▾

ABeginner explanation

A context map is a diagram showing bounded contexts and their relationships. It is more than a box drawing: it records how contexts depend on each other and where their models need translation.

Context Map
A map of bounded contexts and the relationships between them.
Integration point
Where information or work crosses a boundary.

Why it matters: A map reveals coupling and unclear ownership before teams invest in code.

When to use it: Create it during architecture discovery and revisit it when integration or team ownership changes.

BPractical example

An online store maps Ordering, Payments, and Shipping. The arrows name requests or events, not shared database joins.

[Ordering] -- PayRequest --> [Payments]
[Payments] -- PaymentCompleted --> [Ordering]
[Ordering] -- ReleaseDelivery --> [Shipping]

The map exposes which contexts interact and what they exchange.

CInterview question

What would you add to a context map for a legacy ERP, SaaS CRM, and new order system?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would show each model boundary, the owners of key facts, direction of dependencies, API or event contracts, and translations. I would mark legacy semantic mismatch and where an anti-corruption layer is needed. I would validate the diagram with the business and delivery teams.

Trade-off / limitation: A detailed map can become stale, so I would keep it focused on consequential contracts and review it as part of architecture decisions.

Mistake to avoid: A physical deployment diagram alone is not a context map; boxes and arrows need business meaning.

EKey takeaways

  • Maps show relationships between contexts.
  • Label data flows and ownership.
  • Record translations where meanings differ.
  • Maintain the map as contracts evolve.

LESSON 20What are Upstream and Downstream Contexts?▾

ABeginner explanation

Upstream and downstream describe a dependency. An upstream context provides information or a service. A downstream context depends on what it provides. This is not simply the order in which business steps happen.

Upstream
The provider in a particular context relationship.
Downstream
The consumer that depends on that provider.

Why it matters: Knowing dependency direction helps teams coordinate contract changes and prevent surprise outages.

When to use it: Identify upstream and downstream whenever one context consumes another context's API or event.

BPractical example

Inventory provides stock availability to Ordering. For that interaction, Inventory is upstream and Ordering is downstream.

[Inventory / upstream]
        |  StockAvailable
        v
[Ordering / downstream]

The direction describes information dependency; another interaction could reverse the relationship.

CInterview question

How should the Inventory team change its availability API without breaking Ordering?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would identify Ordering as a downstream consumer, agree on required semantics and compatibility policy, use contract tests, and announce changes with a migration window. Inventory owns its internal model; Ordering should not depend on private schema details.

Trade-off / limitation: Consumer input and shared planning reduce breakage but can slow independent changes. Good versioning, telemetry, and limited public contracts balance the cost.

Mistake to avoid: Upstream does not mean 'runs first' in every workflow; a context can be upstream in one relationship and downstream in another.

EKey takeaways

  • Upstream provides; downstream depends.
  • Dependency is about contracts, not chronology.
  • Change contracts with consumers in mind.
  • Direction is specific to the relationship.

L5 · PRINCIPAL ENGINEER / SOLUTION ARCHITECT

Principal Engineer Interview Review

This section is advanced preparation added to the original lessons. The examples are hypothetical architecture exercises, not claims about a real production system. At L5, explain what you choose, what you reject, the risks you accept, and how you make adoption workable across teams.

60–90 second answer structure: Business outcome → boundaries and owners → integration and consistency → failure/security → rejected alternative → phased rollout and success measures.

1. Strategic vs. tactical DDD

Strategic DDD: the big picture

Understand subdomains, find core differentiation, define bounded contexts, assign information ownership, and map upstream/downstream relationships. Use this first to decide where models should differ.

Tactical DDD: inside one boundary

Design domain models, entities, value objects, invariants, aggregates, aggregate roots, and domain events. Use it where complicated rules justify this structure. These choices explain how the model protects behavior.

2. Architecture decision guide: choose based on constraints

Decision Choose it when What it costs / when to avoid
Modular monolith with context-aligned modules Teams need clear ownership but one deployment is acceptable, operations are limited, or cross-module coordination is frequent. Shared runtime and release; requires automated rules to stop direct module-to-module data access.
Independently deployed services Contexts have distinct release cadences, scaling, or teams and can tolerate network and data delays. Failure modes, security, observability, operational cost, and harder cross-service consistency.
Synchronous API A consumer genuinely needs a result before proceeding, e.g. an immediate availability check. Latency and availability coupling. Set deadlines, define errors, and guard overload.
Asynchronous integration event Consumers react after a committed business fact without blocking the sender. Duplicates, lag, ordering, retries, schema evolution, reconciliation and user-facing pending states.
Anti-corruption layer (ACL) Old ERP and new domain use incompatible concepts, codes, or lifecycle meanings. More mapping code and latency. Avoid adding one if semantics already match or the layer becomes a business-policy hub.
Rich aggregate model Important invariants require atomic control and behavior has real complexity. More modeling effort; giant aggregates create lock contention and slow changes.

3. System boundary and data ownership checklist

Ask these questions before deciding that two parts of the enterprise should be separate:

  1. Vocabulary: Does a term like Customer, Payment, Account, or Order mean something different?
  2. Rules: Which context can accept, reject, settle, cancel, or reverse a business action?
  3. Source of truth: Who owns each fact, and which other contexts merely keep a copy?
  4. Consistency: Which data must change in one local transaction? Which status can safely arrive later?
  5. Dependencies: Who is upstream? Which published contract is promised to downstream consumers?
  6. People: Who approves schema changes, handles incidents, and maintains contract tests?
  7. Migration: What does the legacy system still own, and how will ownership be transferred?

4. Scalability, reliability, security, and maintainability

Scalability

Separate compute or deploy only where load and autonomy justify it. Keep aggregates small. Watch lock contention, hot keys, queue delay, and uneven consumer workloads. Model boundaries alone do not guarantee scale.

Reliability

Define timeouts, retry budgets, idempotent handlers, dead-letter handling, replay, and reconciliation. If Payments succeeds but delivery of its event is delayed, show a Pending state—not an invented success or failure.

Security

Authenticate workloads, authorize each cross-context action, minimize sensitive fields in events, encrypt stored and transmitted data, and audit critical decisions. Separate model ownership from access permission.

Maintainability

Test domain rules independently. Publish contract schemas, support compatible change, version deliberately, and enforce module imports/data access in CI. Track who owns legacy translations and when they can be removed.

5. Common architecture anti-patterns

Anti-pattern Why it fails Better approach
Database-table-per-microservice Technical decomposition ignores business invariants and drives chatty cross-service work. Discover capabilities and consistency boundaries before deployment units.
One universal Customer / Order schema Different meanings become a central compromise that every team must coordinate. Use context-specific models with stable IDs and explicit translation.
Shared database writes between contexts Other teams can bypass the owning context's rules. Assign an owner and expose operations through controlled contracts.
Distributed monolith Many deployable services still need lockstep releases or synchronous chains. Improve contract autonomy; consolidate parts if necessary.
Giant aggregates Transactions become slow and contended. Scope aggregates to invariants and coordinate separate aggregates explicitly.
Events treated as guaranteed exactly-once side effects Retries, lost acknowledgments, and duplicate messages cause incorrect outcomes. Use durable publication, consumer idempotency, status tracking, and reconciliation.
ACL becomes a new enterprise brain Translation middleware accumulates independent business rules and ownership confusion. Keep the ACL focused on semantic adaptation.

6. Cross-team technical leadership and implementation

Principal-level work includes decisions and governance, not just architecture diagrams. I would:

  1. Run workshops with domain experts and delivery teams to capture terms, rules, edge cases, and disagreements.
  2. Publish a context map and decision records (ADRs) with ownership, rejected options, and explicit assumptions.
  3. Define minimum contract standards: semantic versioning policy, consumer tests, error handling, observability, and security review.
  4. Use measurable rollout criteria: failed-contract changes, coordinated releases, business-error rate, event lag, incident recovery, and change lead time.
  5. Delegate domain decisions to accountable teams; time-box escalations and record approved exceptions.

Enterprise challenge: teams may disagree about ownership, the legacy database may still be shared, and migration can temporarily increase complexity. Prefer incremental seams with measurable exit criteria instead of a risky all-at-once rewrite.

7. Scenario-based Principal interview questions and model answers

Scenario 1 — Legacy ERP + SaaS CRM + new e-commerce platform

Question: Define model boundaries and select an integration strategy without a big-bang rewrite.

Reveal model answer

I would map CRM relationship management, ordering, invoicing, and payment responsibilities with domain experts. I would identify facts owned by the legacy ERP and avoid assuming all Customer fields belong to one universal model. A context map would show who supplies each fact and who depends on it. For incompatible legacy meanings, I would add a focused anti-corruption layer that translates identifiers and statuses.

Choice: Start with stable APIs or events and an incremental migration by capability. Trade-off: Coexistence creates temporary duplication and reconciliation requirements. Control: Contract tests, audit, dual-run validation where safe, cutover criteria, and a retirement plan for adapters.

Scenario 2 — Payment commits; downstream never receives the event

Question: How would you protect consistency and recover without charging twice?

Reveal model answer

Payments is authoritative for payment status. I would save the payment transition and an outbox message in the same database transaction, then have a relay publish the committed event. Consumers use event IDs to prevent duplicate effects and record progress. If an acknowledgment is lost, we retry delivery safely rather than reprocess the charge blindly.

Choice: Eventual consistency for downstream views, strong consistency at the payment/idempotency boundary. Trade-off: More outbox and consumer operations. Control: Queue-age alerts, reconciliation against authoritative payment records, and clear Pending status for customers.

Scenario 3 — All teams write the same Customer table

Question: Would you split the table into microservices immediately?

Reveal model answer

No. First I would establish meanings: CRM may own contacts and sales relationships, while Billing owns liable party and invoice information. I would inventory all writers and reads and identify transaction dependencies. Then I would make one context authoritative for each business fact, provide a contract, and remove direct writes incrementally.

Choice: Ownership and contract migration before physical decomposition. Trade-off: Temporary adapters and duplicate read models. Control: Change-data validation, consumer migration milestones, security reviews, and a ban on new cross-context writes. Splitting tables first is likely to move existing coupling onto the network.

Scenario 4 — Flash sale with scarce stock

Question: Can Ordering and Inventory always be eventually consistent?

Reveal model answer

Not for every decision. I would identify the invariant that prevents overselling and place the final reservation decision behind the Inventory owner's concurrency controls. Ordering may accept a request as Pending while reservation is checked, then confirm or reject. I would define customer-visible states and cancellation/refund behavior before designing messaging.

Choice: Keep the scarce-stock invariant local to a controlled reservation operation. Trade-off: Confirmation latency or reduced availability under load. Control: Idempotent reservation requests, oversell monitoring, expirations, and reconciliation. An event alone cannot guarantee stock remains available at acceptance time.

Scenario 5 — Two teams disagree on “Active Account”

Question: How would you resolve the semantic conflict without forcing one shared model?

Reveal model answer

I would ask the domain experts to list observable states and decisions. In CRM, Active might mean active sales relationship; in Billing, it might mean permitted to invoice. Both could be valid inside different contexts. I would make the meanings explicit, rename ambiguous contract fields, and translate at the boundary.

Choice: Separate ubiquitous languages, explicit translation. Trade-off: Additional mapping and documentation. Control: Contract examples and tests for edge cases, including suspension, closure, and reinstatement. The mistake is arguing for a single label without examining the business decisions.

Scenario 6 — The CTO wants microservices for every context

Question: How would you challenge the approach constructively?

Reveal model answer

I would agree on desired outcomes first: team autonomy, speed of change, isolation, and scaling. Then I would show two options: enforce bounded-context modules in one deployment or operate separate services. I would compare actual change frequency, required consistency, network failure risk, production support maturity, and ownership.

Choice: Prefer the smallest deployment architecture that meets measured needs. Trade-off: A monolith can limit independent release; services create operational cost. Control: Put architectural checks in CI, record the decision, and define future extraction triggers such as distinct scaling pressure and deployment independence.

Scenario 7 — A regulated bank modernizes payment initiation

Question: How do bounded contexts shape the migration, security, and operational plan?

Reveal model answer

I would separate payment intent and validation, execution/settlement, customer-visible status, and reconciliation according to real ownership. I would confirm exactly which system is authoritative for each payment state. Contracts must define whether Accepted means durable receipt or final settlement. I would use scoped identities, object-level authorization, minimal event payloads, audit records, and idempotency for payment requests.

Choice: Phased API modernization with anti-corruption adapters around legacy engines and resilient status updates. Trade-off: Asynchronous processing adds pending states and reconciliation. Control: Parallel validation where safe, consumer cutover, failure drills, measurable reconciliation exceptions, and rollback plans. Never equate a successful API response with final settlement unless the business contract explicitly says so.

8. Principal readiness rubric

Dimension What a strong answer demonstrates
Business alignment Names a specific outcome, who owns it, and what is out of scope.
Domain boundaries Justifies semantics and ownership rather than technical-only splits.
Alternatives Rejects at least one simpler or more complex option for a concrete reason.
Failure thinking Handles timeouts, duplicates, stale states, recovery, and observability.
Enterprise leadership Explains contracts, ADRs, team collaboration, governance, and rollout metrics.

Self-test: Pick one of the seven scenarios. Hide its answer, speak for 90 seconds, then compare your answer against this rubric. If you cannot name an owner, a failure mode, and an alternative, your answer is not yet L5.
