LESSON 01What is Domain-Driven Design (DDD)?▾

ABeginner explanation

DDD means learning how a business works before deciding how to organize complex software. The software should use business concepts such as Order, Payment, and Shipment, rather than being designed only around tables and frameworks. It helps when many rules and teams must change the system over time.

Domain
The business problem area we are trying to understand.
DDD
A design approach that places business knowledge at the center of software modeling.

Why it matters: If rules are mixed together, one change can accidentally affect unrelated work. Business-centered models make responsibilities easier to discuss and test.

When to use it: Use DDD for complex, changing business rules. For simple CRUD (create, read, update, delete) systems, a simpler design may be enough.

BPractical example

An online shop accepts orders, processes payments, and ships goods. First ask which business decisions each activity makes; only then choose classes, APIs, and databases.

ONLINE SHOPPING
  |-- Ordering: accept / cancel order
  |-- Payments: charge / refund
  `-- Shipping: dispatch / track parcel

The model starts with what the company does, not the cloud product it uses.

CInterview question

You inherit a large retail system. What does applying DDD change about your first two weeks of discovery?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would interview sales, operations, finance, and delivery experts; walk through a purchase and a return; collect business terms and rules; and map decisions and ownership. I would identify areas whose rules change together and areas that need different models. Only after that would I propose modules or services.

Trade-off / limitation: DDD discovery costs time and access to experts. I would use it selectively in rules-heavy areas; straightforward reference-data CRUD may not justify rich modeling.

Mistake to avoid: I would not turn every business noun into a service or claim DDD means microservices.

EKey takeaways

  • Start from business behavior, not infrastructure.
  • Use domain experts to uncover rules.
  • DDD helps with complex domains, not every CRUD screen.
  • DDD is not a synonym for microservices.

What is Domain-Driven Design (DDD)?

A · Beginner explanation

Domain-Driven Design (DDD) is an approach to designing software by first understanding the business and its rules. A domain is the business subject we are solving for, such as online shopping or banking.

Why it matters

It helps software reflect how the business actually works instead of being organized only around screens, database tables, or technical layers.

When to use it

Use DDD thinking when business rules are important, complex, or changing. A simple data-entry application may not need every DDD pattern.

B · Practical example

An online store accepts orders, charges payments, and ships goods. We interview people who do this work, learn the rules of each activity, and then design the application around those business meanings.

ONLINE SHOPPING DOMAIN
  |-- Ordering
  |-- Payments
  `-- Shipping

What this shows: The model starts with what the company does. It does not begin by deciding how many microservices or databases to create.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

You are modernizing an online retail platform. What does DDD change about your first two weeks of architecture work?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Start with domain discovery, not technology selection. Meet ordering, payment, and shipping experts, and map important actions, rules, and terms.
Design
Find major business responsibilities and where rules differ. Capture an initial domain map and check it with business owners.
Trade-off
Domain discovery takes time. For mostly CRUD functionality, use a simpler design instead of enforcing every tactical DDD pattern.
Pitfall
Calling a set of CRUD microservices “DDD” does not make it domain-driven.

E · Key takeaways

  • DDD starts with business understanding.
  • Business rules guide the model.
  • DDD does not require microservices.
  • Choose its depth based on domain complexity.

LESSON 02What is a Bounded Context?▾

ABeginner explanation

A bounded context is a clear boundary within which one business model and its words have specific meanings. Ordering and Shipping may talk about the same purchase, but each needs different rules and information. A boundary tells us which model applies where.

Bounded Context
The scope where a particular domain model is valid.
Model
The concepts, data, and behavior used to represent business work.

Why it matters: It prevents unrelated teams from being forced to share one oversized model.

When to use it: Define contexts when parts of the business use different rules, vocabulary, lifecycles, or ownership.

BPractical example

Ordering manages purchase approval. Shipping manages carriers and delivery status. Both can refer to Order #1001, but they are not required to share all order fields.

[ Ordering model ]      [ Shipping model ]
  Order #1001              Parcel #P77
  buyer, items             carrier, tracking
  confirmed                in transit

One real purchase can appear as different models inside different boundaries.

CInterview question

Must each bounded context become one microservice and one database? Explain.

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: No. A bounded context defines a model boundary, not a deployment unit. I would first identify independent rules, data ownership, and team accountability. Then I might implement the contexts as modules in one application, or as services if independent release and scaling justify the cost.

Trade-off / limitation: Separate services add network latency, distributed failures, security surfaces, and eventual-consistency work. A modular monolith is often the safer first step.

Mistake to avoid: A department, API, database table, and bounded context are not automatically the same thing.

EKey takeaways

  • One context means one coherent model.
  • Contexts can exchange information.
  • Bounded context is not equal to microservice.
  • Choose boundaries from business rules, not tables.

What is a Bounded Context?

A · Beginner explanation

A Bounded Context is a clear boundary within which one particular business model and its vocabulary apply. Inside that boundary, terms and rules have an agreed meaning.

Why it matters

The same real-world thing may mean different things to different parts of a company. A context prevents those meanings from being forced into one giant model.

When to use it

Use it when teams use different concepts or business rules, or one shared model makes changes risky. One context may live inside a monolith; it does not have to be a separate service.

B · Practical example

An Ordering context cares about a buyer, order lines, and order acceptance. A Shipping context cares about parcels, carriers, and delivery stages. Both refer to one purchase, but each needs a different model.

+----------------+   +----------------+
| ORDERING       |   | SHIPPING       |
| Buyer, Order   |-->| Parcel, Route  |
+----------------+   +----------------+

What this shows: The boundary tells each model which business meanings and rules belong inside it.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Would you create one universal Order model shared by Ordering and Shipping? How would you decide?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Do not start with a universal model. Compare language, decisions, rules, and change patterns across the two areas.
Design
Keep separate context models if “order” means different things. Exchange a stable identifier and only the data required by each context.
Trade-off
Context separation creates mapping and synchronization work. A single model may suffice when both teams truly share semantics and lifecycle.
Pitfall
A bounded context is a model boundary, not automatically a service, team, or database.

E · Key takeaways

  • A bounded context defines the scope of one model.
  • One real-world object can have different models.
  • Contexts may communicate.
  • Bounded context ≠ microservice.

LESSON 03What Does Boundary Mean in Bounded Context?▾

ABeginner explanation

A boundary says where a team's model and business responsibilities start and end. Ordering decides if a purchase can be placed or canceled. Payments handles the money movement. Shipping handles delivery. Each has rules it protects.

Boundary
A clear limit around the rules and model a context is responsible for.
Ownership
The team or capability accountable for maintaining the model and its contracts.

Why it matters: A clear limit reduces accidental changes and makes it easier to know whom to ask when a rule changes.

When to use it: Draw boundaries when the same application has responsibilities that evolve for different business reasons.

BPractical example

A laptop is returned. Ordering may decide whether the return meets policy. Payments executes the refund. Shipping arranges the parcel return.

Ordering ---> Payments ---> Refund result
    |
    `----------> Shipping ---> Return label

A workflow may cross several boundaries without transferring ownership of every rule.

CInterview question

Who decides refund eligibility and who transfers the money? Why not put both rules in one shared service?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would assign eligibility to the business capability that owns returns policy and execution to Payments. The workflow coordinates them through contracts. I would verify this with domain experts, because actual businesses may assign returns policy differently.

Trade-off / limitation: More boundaries introduce coordination and failure paths. Splitting two rules is useful only when their meanings, ownership, or changes are truly different.

Mistake to avoid: Do not equate bounded contexts with organizational departments. Teams and systems may cross boundaries.

EKey takeaways

  • Boundaries identify rule ownership.
  • A business workflow can cross several contexts.
  • Ownership must be validated with domain experts.
  • Avoid splitting solely by team chart.

What does “Boundary” mean?

A · Beginner explanation

A boundary separates where one set of business rules is valid from where a different set is valid. Think of it as a responsibility line, not a physical wall.

Why it matters

Clear boundaries reduce the chance that a change in one business area accidentally breaks another.

When to use it

Define them when business responsibilities, policy decisions, or terminology differ. Test proposed boundaries against real workflows.

B · Practical example

Ordering accepts and cancels orders. Payments authorizes charges and processes refunds. Shipping tracks deliveries. The contexts collaborate, but each owns a different decision.

ORDERING: order / return eligibility
PAYMENTS: charge / refund execution
SHIPPING: delivery / tracking

What this shows: Ownership is explicit; communication is still allowed.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

A refund requires an eligibility decision and money movement. Where would you place each rule?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Put refund eligibility where order/return policy is owned; put the money movement in Payments.
Design
Agree on a clear cross-context request or event. Record who makes each decision and which data the consumer can trust.
Trade-off
Two owners require coordination; merging them may reduce calls but mix unrelated rules and lifecycles.
Pitfall
Do not derive boundaries solely from an organizational chart or database table names.

E · Key takeaways

  • A boundary separates model responsibilities.
  • It is logical, not necessarily network-based.
  • Interactions still cross boundaries.
  • Assign decisions to an explicit owner.

LESSON 04Why the Same Word Can Mean Different Things▾

ABeginner explanation

Two business areas can use the same word but mean different things. An 'Order' for Ordering describes what was bought; for Shipping it may be a reference to what must be delivered. DDD lets each context choose the details relevant to its work.

Context-specific meaning
The interpretation of a business word within a particular model.
Semantic mismatch
When two areas use similar words but mean different things.

Why it matters: A universal object with every possible field often becomes hard to change and easy to misuse.

When to use it: Look for different lifecycles, fields, or rules behind familiar words such as Customer, Account, Order, or Status.

BPractical example

Ordering stores products, price, and cancellation state. Shipping stores carrier, parcel tracking, and delivery state. Both may reference Order #1001.

ORDERING: Order {items, price, confirmed}
SHIPPING:  Delivery {orderId, carrier, ETA}

The shared order ID links the work; it does not require identical domain models.

CInterview question

CRM and Billing both store 'Customer'. Should you enforce one enterprise Customer class?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would first compare meanings and lifecycle. CRM may own a prospect and contact relationship; Billing may own the liable payer and invoice address. I would keep distinct domain models if the rules differ, connect them with stable references and explicit translations, and define authoritative ownership for each fact.

Trade-off / limitation: Separate models create mapping and synchronization cost. A small stable shared identifier can help, but sharing the whole object creates change coupling.

Mistake to avoid: An enterprise-wide canonical Customer object is not automatically the cleanest solution.

EKey takeaways

  • Words get meaning from their context.
  • A shared identifier is not a shared model.
  • Avoid a giant universal domain entity.
  • Translate meanings explicitly at context boundaries.

The same word can mean different things

A · Beginner explanation

Within DDD, the same term can have different meanings in different contexts. That is expected; the meanings should be clear within each context.

Why it matters

A universal data model often collects fields and rules that are irrelevant to many teams, making simple changes harder.

When to use it

Use distinct context models when a word such as “Customer,” “Order,” or “Status” has different rules or users.

B · Practical example

Ordering sees Order #1001 as lines, quantity, price, and acceptance status. Shipping sees the same order reference as delivery address, parcels, carrier, and tracking status.

ORDERING: Order #1001 -> items, price
SHIPPING: Order #1001 -> parcels, ETA

What this shows: The order reference connects the views; the internal models do not need identical fields.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

CRM calls someone a “lead,” Ordering calls them a “buyer,” and Billing calls them a “payer.” Should all three share one Customer entity?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Not automatically. First confirm the different meanings and authoritative owners.
Design
Keep context-specific representations and map shared identifiers and essential information at boundaries.
Trade-off
Translation and identity matching add effort. Shared reference data may still be appropriate if semantics are genuinely common.
Pitfall
Treating one enterprise-wide Customer class as the single correct business model everywhere.

E · Key takeaways

  • A word can have different context-specific meanings.
  • Stable IDs can connect different models.
  • Do not share all internal attributes.
  • A universal model can increase coupling.

LESSON 05How Do Bounded Contexts Communicate?▾

ABeginner explanation

Bounded contexts can communicate without using each other's private models. They exchange only the information they need through an agreed interface, such as an API call or a published event.

API
An agreed way for one system to request data or an action.
Contract
The agreed message structure, meanings, and expected behavior.

Why it matters: A stable contract lets one area change its internal implementation without forcing changes everywhere else.

When to use it: Use synchronous requests for immediate answers; use events when other areas can react after a business change.

BPractical example

Shipping finishes delivery and tells Ordering that Order #1001 was delivered. Ordering does not need to know the carrier's route-planning algorithm.

[Shipping] -- DeliveryCompleted(orderId) --> [Ordering]
     private carrier details stay in Shipping

Information crosses the boundary; private shipping rules do not.

CInterview question

When Shipping changes from FedEx to UPS, should Ordering need a release?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: Not if the published contract remains compatible and Ordering depends only on meaningful delivery facts. I would document events or APIs, version them carefully, and use contract tests so teams can release safely.

Trade-off / limitation: Synchronous calls are simple but create runtime dependency; events reduce direct timing coupling but add delay, duplicates, and monitoring needs.

Mistake to avoid: Do not expose internal database tables as the integration contract.

EKey takeaways

  • Contexts communicate via explicit contracts.
  • Keep internal details private.
  • Choose API or event based on timing needs.
  • Test compatibility before changing contracts.

How do Bounded Contexts communicate?

A · Beginner explanation

Contexts work together through explicit contracts: agreed requests, responses, or events. A contract says what information is exchanged and what it means.

Why it matters

It allows one context to change internal behavior while consumers depend on a smaller, stable interface.

When to use it

Use synchronous requests when the caller needs an immediate answer; events when other contexts should react after something has happened.

B · Practical example

Ordering creates Order #1001. Shipping later reports a delivered status through an event or an API. Ordering does not need to know which driver or carrier performed the delivery.

ORDERING --delivery request--> SHIPPING
ORDERING <--delivery result---- SHIPPING

What this shows: The interaction exposes the business outcome instead of Shipping’s internal implementation.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Shipping replaces FedEx with UPS. What must remain stable so Ordering does not need a code change?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Preserve the agreed delivery contract and its meaning where possible.
Design
Expose a delivery status or completion event with a versioned schema and stable order reference; test consumer compatibility.
Trade-off
More abstraction reduces coupling but adds interface maintenance. A direct call is fine for a simple synchronous need.
Pitfall
A shared database table or exposing internal carrier objects to every consumer creates hidden coupling.

E · Key takeaways

  • Contexts integrate through explicit contracts.
  • Hide internal details.
  • Choose API vs event based on use case.
  • Stable semantics matter more than transport.

LESSON 06What is Ubiquitous Language?▾

ABeginner explanation

Ubiquitous Language means business people and developers agree on words and use them consistently within one bounded context. The words appear in meetings, requirements, tests, and code.

Ubiquitous Language
Shared vocabulary within a bounded context.
Domain expert
A person who understands the actual business rules.

Why it matters: A precise shared vocabulary prevents defects caused by misunderstood business terms.

When to use it: Create it during discovery and refine it whenever requirements expose ambiguous terms.

BPractical example

Shipping agrees that 'Shipped' means a parcel left the warehouse, while 'Delivered' means receipt was confirmed. Developers use these meanings in status fields and tests.

Shipped   = handed to carrier
InTransit = moving through network
Delivered = confirmed at destination

Each status has one agreed meaning inside Shipping.

CInterview question

The business says 'Delivered' means received, but the code uses it after dispatch. How do you correct this?

DModel interview answer

Reveal model answer / self-test

Suggested answer: I would bring operations and developers together to define the status transitions, revise the glossary and code names, and create acceptance tests for each transition. If a public contract already uses the incorrect meaning, I would plan a compatible migration rather than silently changing it.

Trade-off / limitation: Shared language needs care as the business changes; not every word must mean the same thing in every bounded context.

Mistake to avoid: Changing a field label without changing the associated rule and contract does not fix semantic confusion.

EKey takeaways

  • Agree on terms with domain experts.
  • Use those terms in code and tests.
  • Different contexts may use different meanings.
  • Protect consumers when changing public status semantics.

L5 · PRINCIPAL ENGINEER / SOLUTION ARCHITECT

Principal Engineer Interview Review

This section is advanced preparation added to the original lessons. The examples are hypothetical architecture exercises, not claims about a real production system. At L5, explain what you choose, what you reject, the risks you accept, and how you make adoption workable across teams.

60–90 second answer structure: Business outcome → boundaries and owners → integration and consistency → failure/security → rejected alternative → phased rollout and success measures.

1. Strategic vs. tactical DDD

Strategic DDD: the big picture

Understand subdomains, find core differentiation, define bounded contexts, assign information ownership, and map upstream/downstream relationships. Use this first to decide where models should differ.

Tactical DDD: inside one boundary

Design domain models, entities, value objects, invariants, aggregates, aggregate roots, and domain events. Use it where complicated rules justify this structure. These choices explain how the model protects behavior.

3. System boundary and data ownership checklist

Ask these questions before deciding that two parts of the enterprise should be separate:

  1. Vocabulary: Does a term like Customer, Payment, Account, or Order mean something different?
  2. Rules: Which context can accept, reject, settle, cancel, or reverse a business action?
  3. Source of truth: Who owns each fact, and which other contexts merely keep a copy?
  4. Consistency: Which data must change in one local transaction? Which status can safely arrive later?
  5. Dependencies: Who is upstream? Which published contract is promised to downstream consumers?
  6. People: Who approves schema changes, handles incidents, and maintains contract tests?
  7. Migration: What does the legacy system still own, and how will ownership be transferred?

4. Scalability, reliability, security, and maintainability

Scalability

Separate compute or deploy only where load and autonomy justify it. Keep aggregates small. Watch lock contention, hot keys, queue delay, and uneven consumer workloads. Model boundaries alone do not guarantee scale.

Reliability

Define timeouts, retry budgets, idempotent handlers, dead-letter handling, replay, and reconciliation. If Payments succeeds but delivery of its event is delayed, show a Pending state—not an invented success or failure.

Security

Authenticate workloads, authorize each cross-context action, minimize sensitive fields in events, encrypt stored and transmitted data, and audit critical decisions. Separate model ownership from access permission.

Maintainability

Test domain rules independently. Publish contract schemas, support compatible change, version deliberately, and enforce module imports/data access in CI. Track who owns legacy translations and when they can be removed.

6. Cross-team technical leadership and implementation

Principal-level work includes decisions and governance, not just architecture diagrams. I would:

  1. Run workshops with domain experts and delivery teams to capture terms, rules, edge cases, and disagreements.
  2. Publish a context map and decision records (ADRs) with ownership, rejected options, and explicit assumptions.
  3. Define minimum contract standards: semantic versioning policy, consumer tests, error handling, observability, and security review.
  4. Use measurable rollout criteria: failed-contract changes, coordinated releases, business-error rate, event lag, incident recovery, and change lead time.
  5. Delegate domain decisions to accountable teams; time-box escalations and record approved exceptions.

Enterprise challenge: teams may disagree about ownership, the legacy database may still be shared, and migration can temporarily increase complexity. Prefer incremental seams with measurable exit criteria instead of a risky all-at-once rewrite.

7. Scenario-based Principal interview questions and model answers

Scenario 1 — Legacy ERP + SaaS CRM + new e-commerce platform

Question: Define model boundaries and select an integration strategy without a big-bang rewrite.

Reveal model answer

I would map CRM relationship management, ordering, invoicing, and payment responsibilities with domain experts. I would identify facts owned by the legacy ERP and avoid assuming all Customer fields belong to one universal model. A context map would show who supplies each fact and who depends on it. For incompatible legacy meanings, I would add a focused anti-corruption layer that translates identifiers and statuses.

Choice: Start with stable APIs or events and an incremental migration by capability. Trade-off: Coexistence creates temporary duplication and reconciliation requirements. Control: Contract tests, audit, dual-run validation where safe, cutover criteria, and a retirement plan for adapters.

Scenario 2 — Payment commits; downstream never receives the event

Question: How would you protect consistency and recover without charging twice?

Reveal model answer

Payments is authoritative for payment status. I would save the payment transition and an outbox message in the same database transaction, then have a relay publish the committed event. Consumers use event IDs to prevent duplicate effects and record progress. If an acknowledgment is lost, we retry delivery safely rather than reprocess the charge blindly.

Choice: Eventual consistency for downstream views, strong consistency at the payment/idempotency boundary. Trade-off: More outbox and consumer operations. Control: Queue-age alerts, reconciliation against authoritative payment records, and clear Pending status for customers.

Scenario 3 — All teams write the same Customer table

Question: Would you split the table into microservices immediately?

Reveal model answer

No. First I would establish meanings: CRM may own contacts and sales relationships, while Billing owns liable party and invoice information. I would inventory all writers and reads and identify transaction dependencies. Then I would make one context authoritative for each business fact, provide a contract, and remove direct writes incrementally.

Choice: Ownership and contract migration before physical decomposition. Trade-off: Temporary adapters and duplicate read models. Control: Change-data validation, consumer migration milestones, security reviews, and a ban on new cross-context writes. Splitting tables first is likely to move existing coupling onto the network.

Scenario 4 — Flash sale with scarce stock

Question: Can Ordering and Inventory always be eventually consistent?

Reveal model answer

Not for every decision. I would identify the invariant that prevents overselling and place the final reservation decision behind the Inventory owner's concurrency controls. Ordering may accept a request as Pending while reservation is checked, then confirm or reject. I would define customer-visible states and cancellation/refund behavior before designing messaging.

Choice: Keep the scarce-stock invariant local to a controlled reservation operation. Trade-off: Confirmation latency or reduced availability under load. Control: Idempotent reservation requests, oversell monitoring, expirations, and reconciliation. An event alone cannot guarantee stock remains available at acceptance time.

Scenario 5 — Two teams disagree on “Active Account”

Question: How would you resolve the semantic conflict without forcing one shared model?

Reveal model answer

I would ask the domain experts to list observable states and decisions. In CRM, Active might mean active sales relationship; in Billing, it might mean permitted to invoice. Both could be valid inside different contexts. I would make the meanings explicit, rename ambiguous contract fields, and translate at the boundary.

Choice: Separate ubiquitous languages, explicit translation. Trade-off: Additional mapping and documentation. Control: Contract examples and tests for edge cases, including suspension, closure, and reinstatement. The mistake is arguing for a single label without examining the business decisions.

Scenario 6 — The CTO wants microservices for every context

Question: How would you challenge the approach constructively?

Reveal model answer

I would agree on desired outcomes first: team autonomy, speed of change, isolation, and scaling. Then I would show two options: enforce bounded-context modules in one deployment or operate separate services. I would compare actual change frequency, required consistency, network failure risk, production support maturity, and ownership.

Choice: Prefer the smallest deployment architecture that meets measured needs. Trade-off: A monolith can limit independent release; services create operational cost. Control: Put architectural checks in CI, record the decision, and define future extraction triggers such as distinct scaling pressure and deployment independence.

Scenario 7 — A regulated bank modernizes payment initiation

Question: How do bounded contexts shape the migration, security, and operational plan?

Reveal model answer

I would separate payment intent and validation, execution/settlement, customer-visible status, and reconciliation according to real ownership. I would confirm exactly which system is authoritative for each payment state. Contracts must define whether Accepted means durable receipt or final settlement. I would use scoped identities, object-level authorization, minimal event payloads, audit records, and idempotency for payment requests.

Choice: Phased API modernization with anti-corruption adapters around legacy engines and resilient status updates. Trade-off: Asynchronous processing adds pending states and reconciliation. Control: Parallel validation where safe, consumer cutover, failure drills, measurable reconciliation exceptions, and rollback plans. Never equate a successful API response with final settlement unless the business contract explicitly says so.
