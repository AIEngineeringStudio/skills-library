Lesson 01 / 20

What is Domain-Driven Design (DDD)?

A · Beginner explanation

Domain-Driven Design (DDD) is an approach to designing software by first understanding the business and its rules. A domain is the business subject we are solving for, such as online shopping or banking.

Why it matters

It helps software reflect how the business actually works instead of being organized only around screens, database tables, or technical layers.

When to use it

Use DDD thinking when business rules are important, complex, or changing. A simple data-entry application may not need every DDD pattern.

B · Practical example

An online store accepts orders, charges payments, and ships goods. We interview people who do this work, learn the rules of each activity, and then design the application around those business meanings.

ONLINE SHOPPING DOMAIN
  |-- Ordering
  |-- Payments
  `-- Shipping

What this shows: The model starts with what the company does. It does not begin by deciding how many microservices or databases to create.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

You are modernizing an online retail platform. What does DDD change about your first two weeks of architecture work?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Start with domain discovery, not technology selection. Meet ordering, payment, and shipping experts, and map important actions, rules, and terms.
Design
Find major business responsibilities and where rules differ. Capture an initial domain map and check it with business owners.
Trade-off
Domain discovery takes time. For mostly CRUD functionality, use a simpler design instead of enforcing every tactical DDD pattern.
Pitfall
Calling a set of CRUD microservices “DDD” does not make it domain-driven.

E · Key takeaways

  • DDD starts with business understanding.
  • Business rules guide the model.
  • DDD does not require microservices.
  • Choose its depth based on domain complexity.

Lesson 02 / 20

What is a Bounded Context?

A · Beginner explanation

A Bounded Context is a clear boundary within which one particular business model and its vocabulary apply. Inside that boundary, terms and rules have an agreed meaning.

Why it matters

The same real-world thing may mean different things to different parts of a company. A context prevents those meanings from being forced into one giant model.

When to use it

Use it when teams use different concepts or business rules, or one shared model makes changes risky. One context may live inside a monolith; it does not have to be a separate service.

B · Practical example

An Ordering context cares about a buyer, order lines, and order acceptance. A Shipping context cares about parcels, carriers, and delivery stages. Both refer to one purchase, but each needs a different model.

+----------------+   +----------------+
| ORDERING       |   | SHIPPING       |
| Buyer, Order   |-->| Parcel, Route  |
+----------------+   +----------------+

What this shows: The boundary tells each model which business meanings and rules belong inside it.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Would you create one universal Order model shared by Ordering and Shipping? How would you decide?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Do not start with a universal model. Compare language, decisions, rules, and change patterns across the two areas.
Design
Keep separate context models if “order” means different things. Exchange a stable identifier and only the data required by each context.
Trade-off
Context separation creates mapping and synchronization work. A single model may suffice when both teams truly share semantics and lifecycle.
Pitfall
A bounded context is a model boundary, not automatically a service, team, or database.

E · Key takeaways

  • A bounded context defines the scope of one model.
  • One real-world object can have different models.
  • Contexts may communicate.
  • Bounded context ≠ microservice.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D · Microsoft Learn M (full source links below).

Lesson 03 / 20

What does “Boundary” mean?

A · Beginner explanation

A boundary separates where one set of business rules is valid from where a different set is valid. Think of it as a responsibility line, not a physical wall.

Why it matters

Clear boundaries reduce the chance that a change in one business area accidentally breaks another.

When to use it

Define them when business responsibilities, policy decisions, or terminology differ. Test proposed boundaries against real workflows.

B · Practical example

Ordering accepts and cancels orders. Payments authorizes charges and processes refunds. Shipping tracks deliveries. The contexts collaborate, but each owns a different decision.

ORDERING: order / return eligibility
PAYMENTS: charge / refund execution
SHIPPING: delivery / tracking

What this shows: Ownership is explicit; communication is still allowed.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

A refund requires an eligibility decision and money movement. Where would you place each rule?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Put refund eligibility where order/return policy is owned; put the money movement in Payments.
Design
Agree on a clear cross-context request or event. Record who makes each decision and which data the consumer can trust.
Trade-off
Two owners require coordination; merging them may reduce calls but mix unrelated rules and lifecycles.
Pitfall
Do not derive boundaries solely from an organizational chart or database table names.

E · Key takeaways

  • A boundary separates model responsibilities.
  • It is logical, not necessarily network-based.
  • Interactions still cross boundaries.
  • Assign decisions to an explicit owner.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 04 / 20

The same word can mean different things

A · Beginner explanation

Within DDD, the same term can have different meanings in different contexts. That is expected; the meanings should be clear within each context.

Why it matters

A universal data model often collects fields and rules that are irrelevant to many teams, making simple changes harder.

When to use it

Use distinct context models when a word such as “Customer,” “Order,” or “Status” has different rules or users.

B · Practical example

Ordering sees Order #1001 as lines, quantity, price, and acceptance status. Shipping sees the same order reference as delivery address, parcels, carrier, and tracking status.

ORDERING: Order #1001 -> items, price
SHIPPING: Order #1001 -> parcels, ETA

What this shows: The order reference connects the views; the internal models do not need identical fields.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

CRM calls someone a “lead,” Ordering calls them a “buyer,” and Billing calls them a “payer.” Should all three share one Customer entity?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Not automatically. First confirm the different meanings and authoritative owners.
Design
Keep context-specific representations and map shared identifiers and essential information at boundaries.
Trade-off
Translation and identity matching add effort. Shared reference data may still be appropriate if semantics are genuinely common.
Pitfall
Treating one enterprise-wide Customer class as the single correct business model everywhere.

E · Key takeaways

  • A word can have different context-specific meanings.
  • Stable IDs can connect different models.
  • Do not share all internal attributes.
  • A universal model can increase coupling.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 05 / 20

How do Bounded Contexts communicate?

A · Beginner explanation

Contexts work together through explicit contracts: agreed requests, responses, or events. A contract says what information is exchanged and what it means.

Why it matters

It allows one context to change internal behavior while consumers depend on a smaller, stable interface.

When to use it

Use synchronous requests when the caller needs an immediate answer; events when other contexts should react after something has happened.

B · Practical example

Ordering creates Order #1001. Shipping later reports a delivered status through an event or an API. Ordering does not need to know which driver or carrier performed the delivery.

ORDERING --delivery request--> SHIPPING
ORDERING <--delivery result---- SHIPPING

What this shows: The interaction exposes the business outcome instead of Shipping’s internal implementation.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Shipping replaces FedEx with UPS. What must remain stable so Ordering does not need a code change?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Preserve the agreed delivery contract and its meaning where possible.
Design
Expose a delivery status or completion event with a versioned schema and stable order reference; test consumer compatibility.
Trade-off
More abstraction reduces coupling but adds interface maintenance. A direct call is fine for a simple synchronous need.
Pitfall
A shared database table or exposing internal carrier objects to every consumer creates hidden coupling.

E · Key takeaways

  • Contexts integrate through explicit contracts.
  • Hide internal details.
  • Choose API vs event based on use case.
  • Stable semantics matter more than transport.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D · Microsoft Learn M (full source links below).

Lesson 06 / 20

Ubiquitous Language

A · Beginner explanation

Ubiquitous Language means business experts and developers agree on the same precise vocabulary inside a bounded context and use it in conversations, docs, tests, and code.

Why it matters

It stops bugs caused by different assumptions about the same word.

When to use it

Build and refine it during domain discovery, story review, design workshops, and implementation.

B · Practical example

The Shipping team defines “Shipped” as “left the warehouse” and “Delivered” as “received by the customer.” A developer must not treat the two as the same status.

What this shows: Shared terms give tests and code precise business meaning. Another context may use different terms.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Two teams disagree about when an order is “complete.” How would you resolve this with DDD?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Ask domain experts to describe what each team needs to decide. Do not impose one global word definition.
Design
Record the exact states and transitions for each context, including examples and acceptance tests. Name cross-context events explicitly.
Trade-off
Maintaining domain vocabulary requires ongoing conversation; documentation alone becomes stale.
Pitfall
Reusing an ambiguous term everywhere even when it hides different business meanings.

E · Key takeaways

  • Agree on terms with domain experts.
  • Use terms consistently in code and tests.
  • Language is scoped to a context.
  • Ambiguous status words cause defects.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 07 / 20

What is a Subdomain?

A · Beginner explanation

A domain is the overall business problem. A subdomain is one part of that business problem, such as inventory management within online retail.

Why it matters

Subdomains help you see the business before making software deployment choices.

When to use it

Find subdomains early during business-capability mapping. Do not assume every subdomain must become a microservice.

B · Practical example

An online shop includes Ordering, Payments, Inventory Management, and Shipping subdomains. Inventory handles stock quantities and availability decisions.

ONLINE SHOPPING DOMAIN
  |-- Ordering subdomain
  |-- Inventory subdomain
  `-- Shipping subdomain

What this shows: This describes what the business does. A bounded context then defines a model boundary for implementing those needs.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Your company has a returns operation and warehouse operations. How would you identify subdomains before drawing services?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Map distinct business responsibilities, not code modules first.
Design
Work with returns and warehouse experts to list decisions, vocabulary, process ownership, and dependencies.
Trade-off
One subdomain may need more than one context, and one simple context may cover closely related work. Align based on real rules.
Pitfall
Equating the business subdomain directly with a deployment unit.

E · Key takeaways

  • Subdomain = part of the business problem.
  • Bounded context = model boundary.
  • Subdomain discovery comes before service design.
  • There is no required one-to-one mapping.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 08 / 20

What is a Core Subdomain?

A · Beginner explanation

A Core Subdomain is a part of the business that gives the company a meaningful competitive advantage.

Why it matters

It helps leaders focus top engineering talent and deeper modeling on capabilities that differentiate the business.

When to use it

Treat an area as core when its unique business rules are central to winning customers; re-check as strategy changes.

B · Practical example

A bank competes by approving loans in five minutes using its own risk process. That unique Loan Risk Assessment capability may be core. A delivery firm competing on ultra-fast delivery may have Delivery Planning as core instead.

What this shows: “Core” depends on the business strategy, not on how technically complicated a function looks.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Why could pricing be core at one retailer but generic at another?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Classify based on how each retailer competes, using product strategy and customer value.
Design
Invest in bespoke modeling where the pricing logic differentiates; use simpler or purchased capabilities where it does not.
Trade-off
Building unique software costs more and requires specialized staff. It is wasted effort if the capability is not differentiating.
Pitfall
Calling every business-critical activity “core.” Critical does not always mean differentiating.

E · Key takeaways

  • Core means competitive advantage.
  • Core varies by business strategy.
  • Invest in understanding its unique rules.
  • Important ≠ core by default.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 09 / 20

What is a Supporting Subdomain?

A · Beginner explanation

A Supporting Subdomain helps the company operate but is not the main source of competitive advantage. It may still need company-specific rules or custom software.

Why it matters

It keeps necessary business functions working while protecting investment in the true core.

When to use it

Use this label when the work is necessary and somewhat specific to the company, but does not distinguish it in the market.

B · Practical example

Our bank’s distinctive risk assessment is core. Its particular loan-document collection process is necessary but might be supporting.

What this shows: Document management matters to operations but is not why customers choose this bank in our scenario.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

A retail company competes on delivery speed but has a custom complaints workflow. How do you classify the complaints function?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Likely supporting unless customer service itself is a deliberate differentiator.
Design
Clarify its unique rules and integrate it cleanly with ordering and delivery without making it a centerpiece of the architecture.
Trade-off
Custom building may be justified by workflow specifics. Buying a generic product may reduce costs if the customization is modest.
Pitfall
Assuming supporting means expendable or low quality.

E · Key takeaways

  • Supporting is important but not differentiating.
  • Some supporting functions need custom rules.
  • Classify according to business strategy.
  • Do not neglect reliability or security.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 10 / 20

What is a Generic Subdomain?

A · Beginner explanation

A Generic Subdomain is a common business problem for which broadly useful solutions already exist.

Why it matters

Reusing a product or managed capability can save time and money that the company can invest in its core subdomain.

When to use it

Consider buying or reusing solutions for common functions, after checking security, cost, integration, and legal requirements.

B · Practical example

A bank needs employee payroll and email. It normally does not gain competitive advantage by inventing its own payroll or email server.

What this shows: Existing systems often solve these widely shared needs, though integration and governance remain necessary.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

An enterprise proposes writing its own employee email platform. How would you assess that proposal?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Treat basic employee email as generic unless a verified special requirement changes that assessment.
Design
Evaluate existing products against security, residency, availability, accessibility, lifecycle, and total cost.
Trade-off
Purchased software introduces vendors and integration dependencies; homegrown software increases development and support burdens.
Pitfall
Generic does not mean trivial, safe by default, or free of customization.

E · Key takeaways

  • Generic = common, well-understood problem.
  • Often buy or reuse instead of build.
  • Check contracts, security, and ownership.
  • Generic does not mean unimportant.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D (full source links below).

Lesson 11 / 20

What is a Domain Model?

A · Beginner explanation

A Domain Model is a simplified representation of important business concepts, facts, actions, and rules. It should describe behavior, not just store fields.

Why it matters

It keeps business decisions understandable and testable instead of scattered across controllers, screens, and database procedures.

When to use it

Model behavior when the domain has real rules and state transitions. Simpler read-only datasets do not always need rich models.

B · Practical example

A Loan Application has an ID, amount, and status. Its model also has an approval operation that checks whether the required risk assessment passed.

What this shows: The model expresses what a valid loan approval means to the bank.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

You see a LoanApplication class with only getters and setters; approval rules are spread across six API handlers. How do you improve it?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Make business behavior and invariants explicit in the domain layer.
Design
Move appropriate state-transition rules into LoanApplication or its aggregate; keep transport, authorization, and orchestration in application-level code.
Trade-off
Rich domain models are valuable for complex behavior but may add ceremony to basic CRUD screens.
Pitfall
Treating a database schema as the full business model or placing every technical concern inside entities.

E · Key takeaways

  • A domain model reflects the business.
  • Behavior and rules matter, not only data.
  • Use it to make valid transitions explicit.
  • Keep infrastructure concerns outside the model.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn D · Microsoft Learn T (full source links below).

Lesson 12 / 20

What is an Entity?

A · Beginner explanation

An Entity is a business object with a stable identity. It remains the same object when some of its attributes change.

Why it matters

Identity lets the business follow one specific object through time and distinguish it from lookalikes.

When to use it

Use an entity when the business cares which specific instance it is, its history, or its lifecycle.

B · Practical example

Loan Application LA1001 changes from Pending to Approved. It is still LA1001. Another application LA1002 could have exactly the same amount and status but is a different entity.

Application LA1001: Pending -> Approved
Identity remains LA1001.

What this shows: Identity, not just matching attribute values, tells us whether it is the same application.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Customer A and Customer B have the same name and address. Can you merge their customer entities?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
No, matching attributes are not enough to establish identity.
Design
Use an identity strategy suitable to the domain and a controlled identity-resolution process before any merge.
Trade-off
Global identifiers ease references but require lifecycle and identity governance.
Pitfall
Using mutable fields, such as customer name, as a permanent unique identity.

E · Key takeaways

  • Entities are defined by identity.
  • Attributes can change without changing identity.
  • Two identical-looking records may be different entities.
  • Identity design deserves deliberate care.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T (full source links below).

Lesson 13 / 20

What is a Value Object?

A · Beginner explanation

A Value Object has no separate business identity. Its meaning comes from its values. Value objects are normally immutable, meaning we replace them rather than change them in place.

Why it matters

Value objects make rules such as money arithmetic, units, addresses, and ranges clearer and safer.

When to use it

Use one when equality depends on the contained attributes rather than a unique lifecycle.

B · Practical example

Two Money values of 50,000 USD are equal regardless of which loan contains them. A value of 50,000 EUR is not equal to 50,000 USD.

Money(50000, "USD") == Money(50000, "USD")
Money(50000, "USD") != Money(50000, "EUR")

What this shows: Amount and currency both matter; the value does not need its own identity.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

You find loan amounts stored as plain decimals in several currencies. What value object would you introduce?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Introduce an immutable Money object that includes amount and currency.
Design
Define equality and allowed arithmetic; reject accidental addition of values in different currencies without an explicit conversion policy.
Trade-off
Additional types require mapping and serialization, but avoid ambiguous primitive values.
Pitfall
Comparing Money using amount alone or treating every value as a uniquely identified entity.

E · Key takeaways

  • Value objects have value-based equality.
  • They do not need separate identity.
  • Prefer immutable values.
  • Money includes currency, not only amount.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T · Microsoft Learn V (full source links below).

Lesson 14 / 20

What is an Invariant?

A · Beginner explanation

An Invariant is a business condition that must remain true whenever the relevant business object or aggregate is in a valid committed state.

Why it matters

It stops invalid business operations even if a user, API, or batch job tries to bypass the expected workflow.

When to use it

Identify invariants for financial correctness, eligibility, limits, quantities, and permitted status transitions.

B · Practical example

In our simplified bank rule, a Loan Application may be Approved only if the required risk assessment has passed. An approval operation must reject an attempt to approve a failed application.

What this shows: The rule is enforced regardless of which interface submits the request.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

An API lets any caller set LoanApplication.status to Approved. What is wrong and how would you fix it?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
A direct status setter bypasses the approval invariant.
Design
Expose a meaningful approve operation that validates required risk evidence before changing state; test both valid and invalid paths.
Trade-off
Some checks require other systems. Define the evidence or decision snapshot that the aggregate can safely trust and how it expires.
Pitfall
Relying only on screen validation; another API or job can bypass it.

E · Key takeaways

  • Invariants protect valid states.
  • Enforce them at the domain boundary.
  • Test invalid transitions, not just happy paths.
  • Do not rely only on UI validation.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T (full source links below).

Lesson 15 / 20

What is an Aggregate?

A · Beginner explanation

An Aggregate is a boundary around one or more domain objects whose important business rules must remain consistent within a single transaction. A transaction means a change set committed together or not at all.

Why it matters

It prevents partial changes from leaving closely related business data in an invalid state.

When to use it

Use an aggregate around rules that truly need one immediate consistency boundary; keep aggregates small.

B · Practical example

An Order contains items and a stored total. If the customer changes a quantity, the system must maintain the rule that the stored total reflects the order lines, in the same commit.

ORDER AGGREGATE
  Order #1001
    |-- Line: laptop, qty 3
    `-- Total: 3000 USD (derived by rule)

What this shows: Order and Order Items are a plausible aggregate for that rule. A separate Shipping aggregate need not be locked during the update.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Would you place Order, Customer, Payment, and Shipping in one giant Aggregate for convenience?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Usually no. Choose the smallest boundary that can enforce required invariants atomically.
Design
Keep Order and its dependent Order Items together when necessary. Reference Customer or Payment by ID and coordinate across them using workflows/events.
Trade-off
Small aggregates reduce contention but require eventual consistency and process coordination across boundaries.
Pitfall
Adding everything that is “related” to a single aggregate, creating oversized transactions.

E · Key takeaways

  • Aggregates enforce transactional consistency.
  • Include only what must change together.
  • Smaller aggregates reduce coupling.
  • Not every relationship belongs inside one aggregate.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T (full source links below).

Lesson 16 / 20

What is an Aggregate Root?

A · Beginner explanation

An Aggregate Root is the main entity that acts as the controlled entry point to an aggregate. Other code should request changes through the root so it can enforce the aggregate’s rules.

Why it matters

It prevents callers from changing child entities in ways that break consistency.

When to use it

Use a root whenever an aggregate has a business lifecycle and internal objects that should not be independently modified.

B · Practical example

Order #1001 is the root. A customer requests “change laptop quantity to 3” through Order. Order validates the change and recalculates the total. Callers do not mutate an Order Item behind the root.

request -> Order.changeQuantity(...)
              |-- validate
              |-- update item
              `-- update total

What this shows: All externally requested changes pass through the same business boundary.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

A developer wants a direct endpoint to update OrderItem.quantity. What design would you recommend?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Route quantity changes through the Order root rather than allowing arbitrary child writes.
Design
Expose an Order.changeQuantity(itemId, quantity) operation and maintain the order total in the same transaction.
Trade-off
The root becomes a concurrency point; if unrelated updates contend heavily, reconsider aggregate size and invariants.
Pitfall
Exposing repositories for child entities that bypass the root’s checks.

E · Key takeaways

  • One root controls each aggregate.
  • Callers change children through the root.
  • The root protects invariants.
  • A root is not automatically a microservice.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T (full source links below).

Lesson 17 / 20

What is a Domain Event?

A · Beginner explanation

A Domain Event is a named fact that something important has already happened in the business, such as OrderPlaced. An event handler is code that reacts to such a fact.

Why it matters

Events make business reactions explicit and help avoid placing every side effect inside one large method.

When to use it

Use them for meaningful changes that other parts of the same domain model need to react to.

B · Practical example

After an Order is placed, it raises OrderPlaced. An application handler can start follow-up work, such as arranging a confirmation notification.

PlaceOrder (command)
   -> Order places order
      -> OrderPlaced (domain event)

What this shows: The event states a past fact. It is not a request like PlaceOrder. A follow-up to an external system may require a separate integration event.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

What makes OrderPlaced a domain event, and what do you do if handling it fails?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Model a business fact in past tense after the underlying domain action is valid.
Design
Define who handles the event and required transaction/consistency semantics; decide whether processing is within the current transaction or eventually consistent.
Trade-off
Handlers improve separation but can hide workflows unless traced and monitored.
Pitfall
Calling every database row update a domain event, or assuming domain events automatically cross service boundaries.

E · Key takeaways

  • Events represent meaningful past facts.
  • They expose reactions explicitly.
  • A command requests work; an event reports what happened.
  • Define handler failure behavior.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T · Microsoft Learn E (full source links below).

Lesson 18 / 20

What is an Integration Event?

A · Beginner explanation

An Integration Event announces a committed business change to another bounded context or application. It is normally delivered asynchronously through messaging infrastructure.

Why it matters

It lets different contexts learn about outcomes without sharing private databases or forcing one long synchronous workflow.

When to use it

Use integration events when another context can respond after the publisher commits and does not require an immediate answer.

B · Practical example

Payments confirms a charge and commits its record. It publishes PaymentCompleted. Ordering receives it and may mark the order as Paid according to its own rules; Shipping might use a downstream fulfillment signal.

PAYMENTS -- PaymentCompleted --> ORDERING
 committed        event        updates status

What this shows: An integration event crosses the context boundary; it should not be emitted as a confirmed fact before the originating transaction succeeds.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Payment commits, then the messaging broker is unavailable. How do you avoid losing PaymentCompleted?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Use a reliable publication mechanism rather than a naïve database-write-then-publish sequence.
Design
Persist payment state and an outbox message atomically, then publish asynchronously. Consumers handle duplicates and track processing failures.
Trade-off
This adds operational cost, monitoring, and eventual consistency. If an immediate response is required, a synchronous API may be simpler.
Pitfall
Assuming a message broker gives exactly-once financial effects or publishing before the state is committed.

E · Key takeaways

  • Integration events cross context boundaries.
  • Publish only committed facts.
  • Plan for duplicate delivery and delays.
  • Outbox can prevent lost publish after commit.

Verified references: Microsoft Learn T · Microsoft Learn E · Microsoft Learn O (full source links below).

Lesson 19 / 20

What is a Context Map?

A · Beginner explanation

A Context Map is a diagram and shared description of bounded contexts and their relationships. It shows which contexts depend on others and how their models connect.

Why it matters

It makes integration points, responsibilities, and change impacts visible to engineering and business teams.

When to use it

Create one when multiple contexts exchange data or services. Update it as contracts, responsibilities, or ownership change.

B · Practical example

An online shopping map shows Ordering requesting payment, Payments providing payment outcomes, and Shipping sending delivery updates. Each arrow should say what is exchanged and who owns the contract.

ORDERING --request--> PAYMENTS
ORDERING <--result--- PAYMENTS
ORDERING <--status--- SHIPPING

What this shows: The map shows relationships, not just a network diagram or list of servers.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

What should a context map contain for a CRM, Ordering, and Billing modernization?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Show the three domain models, ownership, interaction directions, and translation points.
Design
Annotate each relationship: data supplied, API/event contract, owner, upstream/downstream, and where an anti-corruption layer is needed.
Trade-off
Maintaining the map takes effort, but outdated or missing relationships lead to surprising coupling and migration failures.
Pitfall
Producing only a deployment diagram and calling it a context map.

E · Key takeaways

  • A context map shows bounded-context relationships.
  • Label contracts and dependencies.
  • It is not a server topology.
  • Keep it accurate during modernization.

What are Upstream and Downstream Contexts?

A · Beginner explanation

An Upstream Context provides data or capabilities that another context depends on. The dependent context is called Downstream. These labels describe a dependency relationship, not which business step happens first.

Why it matters

Understanding the direction helps teams agree on contracts, change notices, and accountability.

When to use it

Use these labels while reviewing context maps and deciding who must preserve compatibility when interfaces change.

B · Practical example

Inventory provides stock availability to Ordering. For that relationship, Inventory is upstream; Ordering is downstream. A different relationship can reverse their roles.

INVENTORY (upstream)
     | stock availability
     v
ORDERING  (downstream)

What this shows: Ordering depends on Inventory’s published meaning of available stock, so a breaking Inventory contract affects Ordering.

C · Interview question

Answer aloud before revealing the model answer

Inventory changes the meaning of “available.” Who must coordinate and what protections would you require?

D · Model interview answer (advanced)

Decision
Inventory is upstream for availability; Ordering is downstream and at risk from the semantic change.
Design
Have both teams review the contract, version incompatible semantics, run consumer contract tests, and coordinate rollout.
Trade-off
Formal coordination slows changes but reduces production incidents. Small internal systems may use lighter governance.
Pitfall
Assuming “upstream” simply means first in the timeline, or that one context is always upstream in every relationship.

E · Key takeaways

  • Upstream supplies a dependency.
  • Downstream consumes the dependency.
  • The direction is relationship-specific.
  • Protect downstream users from breaking changes.

Boundary rule: A subdomain identifies business work; a bounded context owns one model; a service is a deployment choice. Do not assume these boundaries match one-to-one.

2 · Scalability, reliability, and maintainability

  • Scalability: Measure transaction throughput, p95/p99 latency, hotspots, and team release coupling before splitting services. Small independent contexts can still share a deployment.
  • Consistency: Keep strict invariants inside a small aggregate transaction. Across aggregates or contexts, name the eventual-consistency window and plan reconciliation where needed.
  • Reliability: Design for timeouts, lost acknowledgments, event duplication, broker lag, bad messages, retries, and downstream outages. A “message sent” is not the same as a confirmed business effect.
  • Maintainability: Use stable contracts, ownership maps, consumer contract tests, and compatibility policies. Avoid sharing database schemas or domain entities across teams.
  • Security: Apply authorization per context and data owner, minimize sensitive event payloads, encrypt where needed, keep audit evidence, and manage retention.
  • Operations: Trace a business transaction across contexts by correlation ID, measure age of unprocessed events, alert on reconciliation breaks, and document runbooks.

3 · Real-world implementation challenges

In a legacy ERP/CRM/commerce modernization, the hardest work is often discovering who really owns each business rule, identifying hidden direct database dependencies, and deciding how old and new models coexist during migration.

Practical sequence: interview domain experts → map terms and rules → define candidate contexts → record data and decision owners → identify interactions → choose modular or distributed implementation → implement contract tests and migration controls → measure coupling and operational results.

5 · Cross-team technical leadership

For each context, agree on a domain steward (business expert), engineering owner, published contract, change policy, and escalation path. Run short cross-team modeling workshops with concrete examples. Record meaningful decisions in Architecture Decision Records (ADRs): context, options, chosen approach, rejected alternative, risks, owner, review date.

Govern the boundary standards, not every line of code. Require proof of compatibility and nonfunctional requirements for changes that cross contexts. Allow documented exceptions, assign a retirement date, and inspect evidence rather than relying on a diagram alone.

6 · Scenario-based Principal interview drills

Scenario A — One Customer across CRM, Ordering and Billing

Question: The CIO wants one shared Customer table across all three systems. How do you respond?

Model answer: First establish what “customer” means to each area. CRM may track prospects, Ordering buyers, Billing payers. Identify authoritative identity and attributes; separate context-specific rules; link models by stable IDs and translate at contracts. A master-data or identity service can still provide trusted common identifiers where needed. The shared table seems simpler but creates schema coupling, unclear ownership, access-control risks, and coordinated releases. Validate the design with domain experts and one end-to-end use case before committing.

Scenario B — “Let every bounded context become a microservice”

Question: A program director wants twenty services in the first release. Do you agree?

Model answer: No automatic one-to-one split. First assess separate business lifecycles, team ownership, deployment frequency, scaling needs, data consistency, and operational maturity. I would likely start with a modular monolith if domain knowledge is evolving and releases can remain coupled. Enforce module interfaces and ownership so we can extract selectively. Microservices pay off when independent change and resilience benefits outweigh network, observability, and consistency costs. Record extraction triggers in an ADR.

Scenario C — Payment succeeds but Ordering never hears about it

Question: Payment committed, then the broker went down. How is the order recovered?

Model answer: Payment is the source of truth for the successful charge. Commit its state and an outbox record together; a relay retries publishing. Ordering consumes an idempotent, versioned PaymentCompleted event and acknowledges only after safely recording processing. Monitor broker lag and reconcile payment records against order states. Never reverse or replay financial effects blindly. Event delivery is at-least-once in many practical designs, so duplicate protection matters.

Scenario D — Aggregate performance deteriorates

Question: A Customer aggregate holds all orders and refunds. During peak shopping, locks increase. What do you change?

Model answer: Identify invariants that truly require one transaction. A single customer-root aggregate is likely too wide. Give Order and Refund their own roots and lifecycles, reference customer identity, and use events or an orchestration flow for business processes that span roots. Measure throughput and contention before and after. The cost is eventual consistency and more explicit failure handling; do not split an invariant that genuinely needs atomic enforcement.

Scenario E — Legacy ERP uses confusing order statuses

Question: The ERP’s “Closed” status means billed, but Ordering uses “Closed” to mean no further edits. How do you integrate?

Model answer: Keep Ordering’s meaning intact. Add a narrow Anti-Corruption Layer owned by the consuming context that translates ERP values into explicit domain concepts. Test each semantic mapping, including unknown statuses and future versions. Monitor failures and mapping drift. The ACL adds latency and maintenance but prevents ERP meanings from leaking throughout the new design. Retire or simplify it when migration makes it unnecessary.

Scenario F — Two teams fight over Inventory ownership

Question: Commerce and warehouse teams both want to define “available stock.” What is your process?

Model answer: Facilitate a workshop using real cases: physical count, reserved stock, damaged stock, and promised stock. Identify the decisions each team must make. Define distinct terms where meanings differ and assign each authoritative fact to a clear owner. Put context relationships and upstream/downstream contracts on a map. Negotiate versioning and change windows; escalate unresolved business policy decisions to the accountable product leader. Publish an ADR rather than imposing a purely technical compromise.

7 · A strong 90-second answer structure

  1. Outcome: State the business goal and the unacceptable failure.
  2. Boundary: Name the business model, data owner, and invariant.
  3. Choice: Explain your preferred implementation and why it fits.
  4. Trade-off: Compare a simpler alternative and one rejected option.
  5. Proof: State failure recovery, security, testing, metrics, and phased rollout.

L5 readiness check: You are ready to defend a design when you can explain why this boundary, why this contract, why now, how it fails, how you know it works, and who owns it.
